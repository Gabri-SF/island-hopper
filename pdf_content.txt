Executive Summary
This report presents the conceptual design of a possibly premium Urban Air Mobility (UAM) system for inter-
island transportation in the Balearic Archipelago. The market study identifies a relevant opportunity supported by a
resident population of approximately 1.26 million people and a seasonal tourism flow of about 19 million visitors in
2025. The proposed service targets passengers who value time, flexibility, comfort, and exclusivity, offering a faster
and lower-emission alternative to conventional air, ferry, and helicopter connections.
The reference network uses existing aviation infrastructure and focuses on two routes: Ibiza-Palma de Mallorca,
with a distance of approximately 140 km, and Palma de Mallorca-Menorca, with a distance of approximately 132 km.
The mission is defined as a medium-range VTOL operation carrying one pilot and six passengers with baggage,
corresponding to a preliminary payload of approximately 600 kg. The aircraft must therefore combine vertical take-
off and landing capability, efficient cruise, over-water operational margins, low noise, and compatibility with the EASA
small-category VTOL limits.
Four UAM architectures were benchmarked: Fixed-Wing VTOL, Tilt-Rotor, Tilt-Wing, and Tilt-Duct. An Analytic
Hierarchy Process ranked these options against performance, emissions, comfort, cost, maintenance, and operational
suitability. The Tilt-Duct configuration was selected because it offers a strong compromise between compact vertiport
operation, enclosed-rotor safety, acoustic benefits, distributed electric propulsion, and efficient transition to cruise.
The resulting concept uses six tilting ducted rotors distributed across a canard-wing aircraft layout. Preliminary
sizing with the Aircraft Design Tool and supporting calculations gives a maximum take-off mass of 2595 kg, remaining
well below the regulatory limit. Aerodynamic sizing adopted a 13 m wingspan, aspect ratio of 9, and reference
wing area of approximately 18.78 m2, with the NACA 4412 selected as a robust airfoil for the disturbed flow and
structural requirements of a tilt-duct aircraft. The fuselage was further developed with CAD and OpenVSP, giving
an approximate length of 9.89 m and maximum diameter of 2.2 m.
The mission energy requirement is estimated at approximately 71.66 kWh, with 20.2 kWh required for the vertical
phases, 21.6 kWh for climb, and 32.3 kWh for cruise. A solution relying entirely on battery was found to be highly
sensitive to its specific energy, C-rate, and life cycle, with the optimistic Li-S case requiring about 329.89 kg of
batteries.
The retained solution is therefore a hybrid electric-fuel-cell architecture: a Li-S battery pack supplies
VTOL, hover, and transient power, while a hydrogen PEMFC subsystem supplies cruise and climb energy.
The
preliminary hybrid system totals approximately 197.73 kg, representing a mass reduction of about 40.06% relative to
the optimistic all-electric Li-S case.
Overall, the project supports the technical plausibility of a hybrid tilt-duct UAM aircraft for Balearic inter-island
transport. The concept offers promising mission fit, lower direct operational emissions, improved range scalability,
and a compact VTOL configuration. However, the design remains conceptual and requires further work in detailed
rotor and duct design, CFD, structural validation, hydrogen integration, high-voltage safety, acoustic assessment,
cost refinement, and certification planning.

1
Introduction
1.1
Motivation
This work is inserted in the Project in Aerospace Engineering of the respective Bachelor’s Degree at Instituto
Superior T´ecnico.
The Balearic archipelago offers a compelling opportunity for the study of Urban Air Mobility (UAM). Its geography
creates recurrent transport needs between islands, while the strong seasonal tourism market generates periods of
intense mobility demand. At the same time, the region faces increasing pressure to improve connectivity without
amplifying noise, local emissions, and infrastructure congestion. In a period where aviation is increasingly required
to transition toward cleaner and more efficient solutions, the Balearic case provides a relevant scenario for assessing
whether a premium VTOL service can deliver meaningful emission reduction, time savings and operational flexibility.
The project is therefore not limited to the definition of a route or a vehicle concept. It combines market research,
concept-of-operations definition, aircraft architecture selection, preliminary sizing, aerodynamic analysis, propulsion
and energy assessment, CAD development, stability considerations, emissions, noise, and cost evaluation.
This
broader approach is necessary because the feasibility of an inter-island UAM aircraft depends on the interaction
between mission requirements, aircraft mass, energy storage, vertical-flight power demand, infrastructure constraints,
and certification-oriented safety considerations.
1.2
Objectives
The main objective of this report is to develop a coherent conceptual proposal for an inter-island UAM system
tailored to the Balearic Islands. The project is guided by three main goals:
• To explore the electrification of aviation as a feasible pathway for short to medium-range regional mobility.
• To assess a cleaner alternative for short inter-island flights, where conventional jet-powered aircraft may be
operationally excessive.
• To pursue a configuration with no direct in-flight CO2 emissions, consistent with the long-term transition
toward more sustainable aviation.
The report is therefore organized as a sequence of the work developed throughout the project, starting with the
identification of the operational opportunity and progressing through concept selection, preliminary sizing, aerody-
namic and propulsion analysis, CAD development, stability, emissions, noise, cost assessment, and final conclusions
on the feasibility of the proposed goals.

3
Global UAM Solutions
3.1
Fixed Wing VTOL
The Fixed-Wing VTOL configuration, commonly referred to as the ”Lift + Cruise” architecture, utilizes a de-
coupled propulsion strategy to separate the vertical and horizontal flight regimes. This design paradigm prioritizes
mechanical simplicity and reliability by avoiding the complex actuating mechanisms required to physically rotate the
propulsion units or the airframe.
3.1.1
Technical Characteristics
The defining hallmark of the Fixed-Wing VTOL architecture is the employment of two entirely independent sets
of propulsion systems that remain statically fixed in their respective orientations. The vertical lift system typically
consists of multiple electric rotors mounted on longitudinal booms or directly integrated into the wing structure,
oriented exclusively to provide upward thrust. Conversely, horizontal thrust is generated by a separate, independent
propulsion unit, usually a pusher or puller propeller mounted on the empennage or aft fuselage.
During the operational cycle, the vertical rotors are active exclusively during the takeoff, hover, and landing
phases. Once the aircraft achieves a safe altitude, the cruise motor is engaged to provide forward acceleration. As
the aircraft surpasses the stall speed of its fixed wings, aerodynamic lift fully supports the vehicle’s weight.
At
this precise transition point, the vertical rotors are deactivated. To mitigate aerodynamic penalties, modern designs
typically feature mechanisms that align and lock the stationary rotor blades in a low-drag configuration parallel
to the freestream airflow. This transition relies entirely on software-driven thrust blending rather than mechanical
articulation.
3.1.2
Analysis of Advantages and Disadvantages
The fundamental merit of the Lift + Cruise configuration lies in its exceptional mechanical simplicity and inherent
safety. By eliminating the heavy and complex tilting hinges, gearboxes, and cross-shafting found in tilt-rotor or tilt-
wing designs, this architecture significantly reduces maintenance requirements and simplifies the type certification
process. The static nature of the lift rotors also facilitates a highly redundant Distributed Electric Propulsion (DEP)
network, ensuring that the failure of a single lifting rotor does not critically compromise the hover capability.
However, these operational dividends are counterbalanced by significant aerodynamic and mass penalties during
the cruise phase. The most profound drawback is the parasitic drag induced by the exposed vertical rotors and their
supporting boom structures, even when the blades are locked in an optimal alignment. Furthermore, the vertical
propulsion system becomes essentially ”dead weight” during forward flight, as it contributes nothing to aerodynamic
lift or horizontal thrust. This dual penalty of increased empty weight and elevated cruise drag invariably restricts
the maximum range and payload capacity of the aircraft compared to fully convertible architectures. Consequently,
while highly effective for shorter urban hops, its application for extended inter-island maritime routes requires careful
optimization of the battery mass fraction.
5

=== PAGE 15 ===
3.1.3
Existing Implementations and Concept Designs
Notable implementations of the Fixed-Wing VTOL architecture include the Wisk Aero air taxi (Generation 6),
the Beta Technologies ALIA-250, and the Archer Midnight. These platforms utilize dedicated vertical lifting rotors
combined with an independent pusher propeller for cruise flight. Specifically, the Archer Midnight exemplifies this
with its rear lifting rotors that lock into place during forward flight to reduce parasitic drag. Together, they serve as
prime examples of prioritizing mechanical simplicity and certification viability in modern UAM design.
3.2
Tilt-Rotor
The tilt-rotor configuration represents a sophisticated hybrid class of aircraft designed to synthesize the VTOL
capabilities of a helicopter with the high-speed, long-range cruise performance of a fixed-wing airplane. Indeed, this
architectural paradigm is defined by its ability to reconfigure its propulsion system during flight, allowing it to navigate
the distinct physical requirements of both urban hovering and regional transit cruise.
3.2.1
Technical Characteristics
The primary hallmark of the tilt-rotor is its dual-mode propulsion system. Unlike conventional rotorcraft, which
are aerodynamically constrained by the physical limitations of retreating blade stall and high drag at speed, the
tilt-rotor employs pivotable nacelles. During the hover phase, the proprotors operate in a horizontal plane to generate
lift directly. Upon reaching a sufficient transition speed, the nacelles rotate forward, transforming the proprotors into
propellers. In this mode, the aerodynamic lift is transferred from the rotors to the fixed wings, significantly increasing
operational efficiency. This transition is governed by complex flight control laws that shift aerodynamic responsibility
from the proprotors to the fixed-wing surfaces, thereby enabling operational speeds and altitudes that exceed the
capabilities of traditional VTOL platforms. The inherent versatility of this design allows for the utilization of localized
vertiports within urban centers while maintaining the transit great distances in a relatively fast manner.
3.2.2
Analysis of Advantages and Disadvantages
While the tilt-rotor offers unparalleled advantages in terms of mission radius and cruise velocity, its implementation
is accompanied by significant engineering challenges and performance trade-offs.
The primary benefit lies in its
superior range and the ability to fly at altitudes up to 25,000 feet. Furthermore, the reduction in proprotor RPM
during the cruise phase significantly diminishes noise pollution, which is a vital metric for maintaining a social license
to operate near urban environments.
However, these merits must be weighed against the inherent mechanical complexity of the tilting mechanisms
and the structural weight penalties associated with reinforced wing designs required to support rotating mass at the
extremities. These factors often result in a reduced payload-to-weight ratio and higher maintenance requirements
compared to simpler multicopter or fixed-wing VTOL configurations. Additionally, the high disc loading characteristic
of tilt-rotors generates intense downwash during hover operations, which necessitates specialized vertiport infrastruc-
ture to mitigate ground-level environmental disturbances and ensure the safety of ground personnel. Consequently,
while the tilt-rotor remains one of the most capable configurations for long-range transport, its adoption is contingent
upon overcoming the fiscal and technical hurdles.

3.3
Tilt-Wing
The Tilt-Wing configuration represents an advanced propulsive architecture that bridges the gap between rotary-
wing VTOL capability and high-efficiency fixed-wing cruise. The distinguishing feature of this architecture is that
the entire wing assembly, including all integrated propulsion units and their associated nacelles, rotates around a
transversal, span-wise axis at the wing root.
3.3.1
Technical Characteristics
Unlike standard tilt-rotor configurations, where only the engine nacelles rotate at the wingtips, the tilt-wing
designs the wing chord to always align with the resulting force vector of the propellers. The primary aerodynamic
advantage of the tilt-wing lies in the effective management of the propeller-wing aerodynamic coupling, known as the
propeller slipstream interaction. By pivoting the whole lifting surface, the high-energy, turbulent airflow generated
by the propellers (the slipstream) is forced directly over the entire wing chord.
This effect guarantees that the
airflow remains attached to the aerodynamic surfaces throughout the critical transition phase. Even at exceptionally
high angles of attack during takeoff, where a fixed wing would immediately stall, the forced slipstream maintains
aerodynamic control and lift production. This interaction results in a smoother, more continuous lift hand-off between
the propellers and the wing as the aircraft gains forward velocity.
3.3.2
Analysis of Advantages and Disadvantages
The tilt-wing configuration is often selected for missions requiring high aerodynamic cruise efficiency and moder-
ate ranges. The critical performance benefits include aerodynamic transition efficiency and the elimination of hover
download. Because the wing structure is not placed beneath the rotor downwash in hover, nearly 100% of the pro-
peller’s thrust is utilized for vertical lift. Furthermore, the vertical wing profile results in a more compact aerodynamic
ground footprint.
Conversely, the largest disadvantage is the extreme vulnerability of the aircraft to lateral wind gusts (crosswinds)
during the transition phase. With the large surface area of the wing facing the relative wind, significant yaw and roll
moments are generated. Rotating the entire wing assembly also requires highly complex, high-torque, and redundant
actuation systems at the wing-fuselage joint, adding substantial weight. Lastly, as the wing rotates, the center of
pressure shifts significantly, creating a massive pitching moment that the aircraft must counter with its flight surfaces,
such as a T-tail, or through thrust vectoring.
3.3.3
Existing Implementations and Concept Designs
While historically limited by mechanical complexity, the advent of Distributed Electric Propulsion (DEP) has made
the tilt-wing architecture a viable option for modern UAM applications.

configuration (both front and rear wings rotated). Equipped with eight independent electric motors, it successfully
proved the viability of software-driven electric tilt-wing transitions, proving that DEP could replace heavy mechanical
linkages.

its T-tail to actively counter the massive pitching moments generated during the transition phase.
3.4
Tilt-Duct
The tilt-duct configuration represents an advanced development of vectored-thrust propulsion, in which the propul-
sive units are enclosed within aerodynamically refined ducts or shrouds. In this arrangement, the rotors are integrated
into movable housings capable of transitioning between vertical and horizontal orientations, thereby enabling the
generation of both lift and forward thrust within a unified system. Consequently, this approach reduces the aerody-
namic inefficiencies typically associated with open-rotor configurations, while also contributing to a more compact
and spatially efficient airframe design. Within the broader context of contemporary aerial mobility, the tilt-duct con-
cept emerges as a particularly suitable solution for high-frequency operations. This is especially relevant in scenarios
where ground-level safety and noise containment are of primary concern, as the ducted structure inherently supports
improved acoustic management and enhanced operational safety.
3.4.1
Technical Characteristics
The defining feature of the tilt-duct architecture lies in its reliance on ducted-fan propulsion, most commonly
implemented through electrically driven fans, which exploit the aerodynamic effect of pressure recovery at both the
inlet and outlet of the duct to enhance static thrust generation. In contrast to the open-rotor arrangement of tilt-rotor
systems, the presence of the duct serves to confine the airflow, thereby reducing tip-vortex losses and enabling higher
disc loading within a comparatively smaller rotor diameter. As the aircraft transitions from vertical to forward flight,
these ducted units rotate to align with the flight direction and subsequently operate as efficient propulsion pods
during cruise conditions.
Moreover, many contemporary configurations incorporate DEP systems, whereby multiple smaller and indepen-
dently controlled ducted fans are employed, frequently positioned along the trailing edge of the wing. This approach
not only provides redundancy in lift generation but also allows for refined control through differential thrust. In addi-
tion, the integration of these ducted elements with the wing structure enables the ducts themselves to contribute to
lift production during forward flight, which in turn improves the overall lift-to-drag ratio and aerodynamic efficiency
of the airframe.
3.4.2
Analysis of Advantages and Disadvantages
The tilt-duct configuration offers a distinct set of operational attributes, though it introduces some specific
mechanical and physical constraints. The primary merits of the tilt-duct reside in its augmented safety and compact
spatial profile, as the shrouds insulate ground personnel from rotating blades and facilitate a reduced total airframe
span, a necessity for navigating the restricted geometries of urban vertiports.
Furthermore, the duct assembly
provides acoustic attenuation, effectively sequestering noise and refracting it away from receptors, thereby fostering
greater public receptivity. From a performance perspective, the architecture enables optimized cruise proficiency at
moderate velocities, as the duct geometries diminish tip-vortex losses and can be engineered to generate supplemental
9

=== PAGE 19 ===
aerodynamic lift during the translational flight phase.
Conversely, these dividends are countered by substantial impediments, most notably the intensified mass and
parasitic drag inherent in the duct structures themselves, where the additional weight of the shrouds frequently results
in a higher empty mass. The configuration also needs intricate thermal regulation protocols, as the encased electric
motors and power electronics are deprived of the passive cooling flow enjoyed by exposed propellers. Additionally,
while maintaining efficiency during cruise, tilt-ducts frequently demonstrate diminished hover performance due to
the elevated power demands necessitated by high disc loading, which can precipitate rapid energy depletion during
protracted takeoff or landing sequences. Finally, the mechanical intricacy involved in synchronizing a multitude of
tilting units constitutes a profound barrier to type certification, particularly regarding the verification of autonomous
flight control architectures and the mitigation of system failures.

between the Balearic Islands, reducing travel time compared to existing transport modes while simultaneously alle-
viating congestion in current infrastructure. According to the analysis presented in section 2.2, the reference routes
correspond to Ibiza-Palma de Mallorca (approximately 140 km) and Palma de Mallorca-Menorca (approximately
132 km). Consequently, the aircraft must be designed to perform short- to medium-range inter-island missions with
high operational frequency and vertical take-off and landing (VTOL) capability.
Based on the preliminary study, the reference mission consists of transporting six passengers and one pilot, includ-
ing baggage, resulting in an estimated payload near the 600kg. The vehicle is expected to operate typically between
an altitude of 1500 to 3000 m, representing a compromise between aerodynamic efficiency, energy consumption, and
operational flexibility. Importantly, mission performance must not be assessed solely under ideal conditions; instead, it
must incorporate margins to account for adverse winds, holding patterns, missed approaches, and potential diversions.
4.1.1
Payload
The payload is one of the most influential parameters in the system definition, as it directly affects energy re-
quirements, battery sizing, and propulsion power. From a regulatory standpoint, the aircraft must comply with EASA
requirements for small-category VTOL aircraft; in particular, the maximum certificated take-off mass (MCTOM)
should not exceed 5700 kg [12]. At this preliminary stage, an allowable center-of-gravity variation of approximately
7% is also assumed, ensuring that different passenger and baggage configurations do not compromise stability or
controllability.
Beyond its nominal value, payload variability plays a critical role in operational feasibility. Each mission may
involve different combinations of passengers, baggage, and initial energy levels. Therefore, payload should be treated
not only as a commercial parameter but as a key design variable. An increase in payload generally leads to higher
power demand during hover and climb phases, while simultaneously reducing the energy margin available for reserves
and contingencies.
4.1.2
Flight Phase Parameters
The most demanding route identified is approximately 140 km. To complete this mission within 30 to 40 minutes,
the required average block speed lies between 210 and 280 km/h. However, this value includes all flight phases-hover,
transition, climb, cruise, descent, approach, and landing-implying that the cruise speed must be higher, realistically
within the range of 250-350 km/h.
Energy-intensive phases, particularly hover, initial climb, and terminal descent, require careful consideration. These
segments typically account for a significant portion of total energy consumption, even if their combined duration is
on the order of 10 minutes. As a result, aircraft performance cannot be evaluated solely based on cruise efficiency or
range; a complete block-time energy breakdown is essential.
12

=== PAGE 22 ===
The selected operational altitude range of 1500-3000 m reflects a trade-off between competing effects. Higher
altitudes generally reduce aerodynamic drag and improve cruise efficiency, but require additional energy during climb,
which may offset overall gains. Therefore, the optimal altitude must result from a joint optimization of mission time,
energy consumption, and operational margins.

Mission Profile
With these parameters in mind, a set mission profile is established:

4.1.4
Safety Strategies
Given the inter-island nature of the operation, safety considerations must extend beyond nominal mission exe-
cution. The aircraft must retain sufficient energy reserves to accommodate degraded scenarios, including missed
approaches, diversions to alternate landing sites (divert), holding patterns, and extended hover operations. In this
study, a preliminary energy reserve of 30-40% of the total mission energy is considered appropriate to ensure opera-
tional robustness.

This requirement has direct implications for aircraft sizing. Increased payload reduces the fraction of energy
available for reserves, requiring a careful balance between commercial capacity and safety margins. Accordingly, the

minimum operational requirements under disturbances or environmental uncertainties.
4.2
Concept Sketches
Aware of all the conditions and equipment that already exist, the team started to draw initial designs for the
proposed VTOL aircraft. In order to obtain the best results possible, different configurations were put in perspective.
The following sections detail the technical characteristics and operational mechanics of the four primary architectures
selected for evaluation: the Tilt-Rotor, Tilt-Wing, Tilt-Duct, and Fixed-Wing + VTOL.
4.2.1
Tilt-Rotor Configuration
The Tilt-Rotor architecture is defined by a stationary fixed-wing structure equipped with articulating nacelles
mounted at the wingtips. These nacelles house the primary power plants and proprotors. During vertical takeoff and
landing operations, the nacelles are oriented perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the fuselage, allowing the rotors
to generate vertical lift.
The transition to forward flight involves a mechanized rotation of the nacelles through a 90-degree arc. During
this conversion phase, the aircraft gradually shifts the lift requirement from the rotors to the fixed-wing assembly as
forward airspeed increases. Once the nacelles reach a fully horizontal orientation, the proprotors act as traditional
propellers, providing forward thrust while the wings provide the necessary aerodynamic lift for cruise. Control during
hover is typically managed through differential collective pitch and nacelle tilt, while conventional flight surfaces take
over during high-speed flight (Figure 4.2).
Figure 4.2: Tilt-Rotor Configuration
4.2.2
Tilt-Wing Configuration
The Tilt-Wing configuration utilizes a design where the entire wing assembly, including all integrated power plants
and propellers, rotates around a central span-wise axis. For vertical flight operations, the wing is tilted to a vertical
position so that the chord line is aligned with the vertical axis of the aircraft. This directs the full thrust of the
propellers downward to facilitate liftoff and hovering maneuvers.
14

=== PAGE 24 ===
As the aircraft transitions to cruise, the entire wing is incrementally lowered to a horizontal position. A defining
characteristic of this mechanism is that the propellers remain aligned with the wing’s leading edge throughout the
entire rotation. This ensures that the propeller slipstream always flows over the wing surface, which helps maintain
attached airflow (Figure 4.3).
Figure 4.3: Tilt-Wing Configuration
4.2.3
Tilt-Duct Configuration
The Tilt-Duct architecture utilizes ducted fans −rotors enclosed within aerodynamic shrouds −mounted on
pivoting mechanisms.
These units are distributed across the airframe to provide balanced thrust.
For vertical
maneuvers, the ducts are oriented to exhaust air directly toward the ground. The duct geometry is designed to
facilitate a high-pressure differential, contributing to the total lift generated by the propulsion unit.
In the transition to forward flight, the duct assemblies pivot forward to provide horizontal propulsion. The shrouds
serve as physical containment for the rotor blades and function as aerodynamic surfaces that can generate a degree of
lift and stability in forward flight. Control is typically maintained through a combination of motor RPM modulation,
thrust vectoring via the pivoting ducts, and internal stator vanes that deflect the exhaust flow to manage yaw and
roll during low-speed flight. This configuration prioritizes a compact footprint and reduced acoustic signature.
Figure 4.4: Tilt-Duct Configuration
4.2.4
Fixed-Wing + VTOL Configuration
The Fixed-Wing + VTOL configuration utilizes a decoupled propulsion strategy. This design employs two separate
sets of motors that remain fixed in their respective orientations. The vertical lift system consists of multiple rotors
mounted on longitudinal booms or integrated into the wing structure, oriented solely for upward thrust. A separate,
15

=== PAGE 25 ===
independent propulsion unit −usually a pusher or puller propeller −is mounted to the airframe to provide horizontal
thrust.
During the flight cycle, the vertical rotors are activated exclusively for takeoff, hover, and landing. Once the
aircraft reaches a safe altitude, the cruise motor provides forward acceleration. When the aircraft surpasses the
stall speed of the fixed wing, the wing assumes the full lift load, and the vertical rotors are deactivated. In many
implementations, these lift rotors are then locked in a low-drag alignment to streamline the aircraft for efficient
long-distance cruise. This architecture relies on software-driven transitions rather than mechanical tilting hinges.

Structural and Integration Realities of the Tilt-Duct Configuration
While the NACA 64-412 profile presents a superior theoretical aerodynamic efficiency during clean cruise con-
ditions, its selection is ultimately invalidated by structural constraints and the unique operational environment of a
25

=== PAGE 35 ===
tilt-duct aircraft.
First of all, geometrical comparison reveals that the NACA 64-412 section features a significantly thinner trailing
edge compared to the NACA 4412. Structurally, the wing must support the ducts mechanisms mounted at the
tip stations. A thin trailing edge compromises the torsional stiffness and localized shear strength required to safely
transfer these massive concentrated loads along the wing.
Furthermore, the presence of a tilt-duct propulsion unit introduces severe aerodynamic interactions. The physical
duct housings and rotating blades generate high levels of localized turbulence and non-uniform inflow velocity fields
across the span.
Natural laminar flow profiles, such as the NACA 6-series, rely on highly undisturbed pressure
gradients to maintain a laminar boundary layer [7]. The turbulent wake and vibration induced by the ducts would
trigger premature boundary layer transition, completely destroying the low-drag benefits of the NACA 64-412 and
potentially leading to localized flow separation or lift deficits.
Based on these combined aerodynamic and structural integration criteria, the NACA 4412 (A.3.4) was selected
as the final airfoil profile for the primary wing structure.

The geometric dihedral angle (Γ) is defined as exactly 0◦across the entire span.
While positive dihedral is
traditionally utilized in conventional aircraft to provide passive lateral stability (roll-subsidence via slip effects), it
introduces a severe geometric penalty for a tilt-duct VTOL configuration.
During vertical takeoff, landing, and hover transition phases, the tip-mounted propulsion units must rotate into
27

=== PAGE 37 ===
a vertical orientation to generate pure upward lift.
If the wing possessed a non-zero dihedral angle, the thrust
vectors of the tip-mounted rotors would be canted outwards relative to the main aircraft vertical axis during vertical
flight modes. This misalignment would create parasitic horizontal force components, inducing severe control cross-
coupling, reducing net vertical lifting efficiency, and causing control system instability during ground-effect hovering.
A dihedral angle of Γ = 0◦guarantees that the propulsive rotors remain in a perfectly synchronized horizontal plane
during vertical operations, maximizing vertical force and streamlining control effort.

7
Propulsion System and Energy Analysis
The propulsion system is a natural follow-up of the aerodynamic analysis.
Although the present assessment
remains conceptual, it is central to the feasibility of the proposed aircraft, since it determines whether the reference
mission can be achieved with current or near-future technology while preserving the objective of a cleaner inter-island
transport solution.
The key design decision is the selection of the propulsion and energy-storage architecture. The propulsion can
be roughly categorized into combustion and electric solutions. Given the main goals of this projected, a combustion
engine is not desirable. Regarding the energy storage system, there are broader solutions including aviation fuel,
batteries, fuel cells and their specific subcategories.
The analysis therefore considers the energy required by the mission, the mass and performance implications of
different technological setups, and the extent to which the final configuration remains viable in terms of power, weight
and operational endurance.

7.3
Hybrid Electric-Fuel Cell
As shown in the previous section, the fully electric concept is technically attractive but remains strongly constrained
by the mass, cycle life, and discharge capability of the battery system. The natural next step is therefore to consider
a hybrid architecture that preserves electric propulsion at the rotors while reducing the energy-storage burden placed
32

=== PAGE 42 ===
entirely on the battery pack.
Three broad hybrid propulsion architectures were considered: turboelectric, serial hybrid, and parallel hybrid. A
turboelectric system is the least attractive option for the present mission, since it is formally hybrid but remains very
close to a combustion-based architecture in practice. This conflicts with the low-emission objective of the project and
does not make full use of the distributed electric propulsion layout. A parallel hybrid arrangement was also considered,
but it introduces major integration difficulties for a six-duct configuration. Transmitting mechanical power to each
duct would either require six combustion engines, one per duct, or a complex shaft-and-gearbox system. Both options
would increase mass, noise, maintenance costs, and failure points.
The preferred architecture is therefore a serial hybrid electric system. In a conventional serial hybrid, a combustion
engine drives a generator, and the generated electrical power is either supplied directly to the electric motors or used
to recharge the batteries.
In the present design, this concept is adapted into a hydrogen fuel-cell architecture:
the combustion engine and generator are replaced by an electrochemical reactor, while the fuel tank is replaced
by a compressed hydrogen storage system. The rotors remain fully electric, so duct control is decoupled from any
mechanical shaft power and can be managed directly through the motor controllers.
From an energy-management perspective cruise is the longest segment but has a relatively low and steady power
requirement. Climb has moderate power needs due to some of the weight being, at this point, compensated by
the lift generated in the respective surfaces. Finally vertical take-off, hover, and landing are shorter but much more
power-intensive. For this reason, the proposed hybrid strategy uses the fuel-cell system as a steady power source
sized primarily for cruise and climb, while the battery supplies the high transient power required during VTOL and
hover. This division allows each subsystem to operate closer to its most favorable regime.

7.4
Rough Avionics and Motor Choices
The architecture selection has direct implications for the avionics and control system. Since the preferred con-
figuration is a series hybrid electric architecture, the six ducted rotors remain electrically driven and independently
controllable. This is a major advantage over a parallel hybrid configuration, because no mechanical shafting or gear-
box network is required to distribute power to the ducts. Each motor can be commanded individually, preserving the
control authority needed for hover, transition, and low-speed flight.
For the propulsion units, the EMRAX 348 Medium Voltage motor with Combined Cooling (CC) was selected.
This choice is primarily driven by its high operational limit of 800 Vdc, which reduces voltage drops, improves overall
electrical efficiency, and allows for a reduction in cable cross-section, saving wiring mass. The motor delivers a peak
power of 260 kW, safely exceeding the 176 kW worst-case requirement for the critical 30-40 seconds of sea-level
hover. Furthermore, its ability to generate up to 1000 Nm of peak torque enables a direct-drive configuration. By
eliminating the gearbox, the design drastically reduces mechanical complexity and structural weight. Finally, the
compact physical dimensions of the motor, 348 mm in outer diameter and 107 mm in axial width, allow for seamless
aerodynamic integration within the duct’s central spinner.

7.6
Powertrain Architecture Overview
Figure 7.2: 2D Powertrain configuration.
The aircraft employs a hybrid hydrogen-electric propulsion architecture designed to combine the high specific
energy of hydrogen with the high specific power capability of advanced Lithium-Sulfur (Li-S) batteries. This approach
addresses one of the primary challenges of regional Urban Air Mobility operations: providing sufficient energy for a
150 km inter-island mission while maintaining acceptable aircraft mass and preserving the high power levels required
during vertical flight.
The architecture is organized around an 800 V DC high-voltage distribution network that serves as the electrical
backbone of the aircraft. Energy is supplied by two complementary sources: a 200 kW Proton Exchange Membrane
Fuel Cell (PEMFC) system fed by a 700 bar compressed hydrogen tank, and a Li-S battery pack sized to satisfy peak-
36

=== PAGE 46 ===
power demands and emergency reserve requirements. Both systems are connected to a common DC bus, enabling
dynamic power sharing according to mission requirements.
The resulting configuration combines the endurance
advantages of hydrogen propulsion with the rapid response and redundancy offered by battery storage, thereby
providing a balanced solution for the demanding operational profile of a tilt-duct UAM aircraft.
7.6.1
Energy Flow and Operating Philosophy
The energy management strategy is based on the complementary operation of the fuel-cell and battery subsystems
throughout the mission. During vertical take-off, hover, and transition phases, propulsion power demand reaches its
maximum value. Under these conditions, the battery pack provides the majority of the instantaneous power required
by the six propulsion units, while the fuel cell contributes continuous baseline power. This strategy avoids excessive
fuel-cell sizing, reduces overall system mass, and allows the electrochemical conversion system to operate near its
optimal efficiency point.
As the aircraft transitions into forward flight and reaches cruise conditions, power demand decreases substantially
and the fuel cell becomes the primary energy source. The battery then assumes a supporting role, supplying transient
loads, stabilizing the electrical network, and preserving sufficient energy reserves for contingency operations. During
descent, power requirements decrease further, allowing the fuel-cell system to sustain the majority of onboard electrical
loads while maintaining battery availability for the final VTOL segment. This operating philosophy maximizes overall
efficiency while ensuring adequate redundancy and reserve energy under both nominal and off-design operating
conditions.
7.6.2
Hydrogen Energy Subsystem
The hydrogen subsystem is responsible for providing sustained power during cruise flight. Hydrogen is stored in a
lightweight composite pressure vessel operating at approximately 700 bar, selected as a compromise between storage
density, technological maturity, and aerospace integration requirements.
The stored hydrogen is supplied to a 200 kW PEMFC stack, where it reacts electrochemically with oxygen
extracted from ambient air. This process generates electrical power with water and heat as the only by-products.
Compared with conventional combustion-based propulsion systems, the fuel cell eliminates direct carbon emissions
while achieving higher conversion efficiencies and substantially lower acoustic signatures.
To ensure stable operation across the full flight envelope, the fuel-cell subsystem incorporates an air-compression
and humidification unit responsible for maintaining cathode pressure and membrane hydration. In addition, a ded-
icated thermal management system regulates operating temperatures and dissipates waste heat generated during
electrochemical conversion. The electrical output of the fuel-cell stack is conditioned through a DC/DC converter
before being delivered to the high-voltage bus. Since fuel-cell voltage varies with load demand and operating con-
ditions, this converter ensures a stable electrical interface between the hydrogen subsystem and the aircraft power
network.
7.6.3
Electrical Energy Storage Subsystem
A Li-S battery pack is integrated directly into the high-voltage distribution network. Li-S technology was selected
due to its superior theoretical specific energy compared with conventional lithium-ion chemistries, offering significant
mass reductions for a given energy capacity and making it particularly attractive for aerospace applications.
37

=== PAGE 47 ===
The battery system performs multiple functions within the powertrain architecture. Beyond supplying peak power
during VTOL operations, it acts as an electrical buffer capable of absorbing rapid load fluctuations and supporting
transient operating conditions. Furthermore, the battery provides a critical layer of redundancy by ensuring continued
propulsion capability in the event of fuel-cell degradation or temporary power deficits.
Battery operation is supervised by a dedicated Battery Management System (BMS), which continuously monitors
cell voltages, temperatures, current flow, and state-of-charge. Through continuous health monitoring and protective
functions, the BMS ensures safe operation throughout all phases of flight while maximizing battery performance and
service life.
7.6.4
Power Distribution and Control Architecture
Power distribution throughout the aircraft is achieved through a centralized 800 V DC bus, which serves as
the primary electrical interface between the energy sources and propulsion system. The adoption of a high-voltage
architecture is primarily motivated by efficiency considerations, as it enables the transmission of large amounts of
electrical power while minimizing conductor mass and resistive losses.
Supervision of the entire powertrain is performed by the Vehicle Control Unit (VCU), which functions as the
central energy management controller. The VCU continuously determines the optimal power split between the fuel-
cell and battery subsystems while monitoring system health, thermal conditions, fault states, and available energy
reserves. This centralized control philosophy enables efficient operation of the hybrid architecture while ensuring
compliance with safety and reliability requirements.
7.6.5
Propulsion System
Propulsive power is delivered to six distributed tilt-duct propulsion units through dedicated Electronic Speed
Controllers (ESCs) and EMRAX 348 Medium Voltage electric motors.
The ESCs convert DC electrical power
from the high-voltage bus into controlled three-phase AC power while providing precise torque and rotational speed

The EMRAX 348 motor was selected due to its exceptional power density, high efficiency, compact dimensions,
and proven suitability for aerospace applications. Each motor directly drives a tilt-duct propulsion unit, eliminating
the need for complex gearbox arrangements and thereby improving mechanical reliability while reducing maintenance
requirements.
The distributed propulsion architecture provides both vertical lift and forward thrust depending on rotor orien-
tation. In addition to enhancing operational flexibility, the use of six independent propulsion units improves fault
tolerance and system redundancy, key requirements for future certified UAM operations.
7.6.6
Powertrain Integration and Component Placement
The physical arrangement of the powertrain components was primarily driven by four design objectives: min-
imization of high-current cable lengths, optimization of aircraft center-of-gravity (CG) location, efficient thermal
management and proper eletromagnetic comptability.
The battery pack, fuel-cell system, DC/DC converter, and Vehicle Control Unit were therefore concentrated
within the central fuselage section, close to the aircraft center of gravity. Since the battery and hydrogen subsystems
represent the largest masses within the powertrain, locating them near the CG minimizes stability variations resulting
38

=== PAGE 48 ===
from fuel consumption and battery state-of-charge changes throughout the mission. Furthermore, central placement
reduces structural bending moments and facilitates maintenance access.
The hydrogen storage tank was positioned immediately aft of the passenger compartment and adjacent to the
fuel-cell system. This arrangement minimizes hydrogen feed-line lengths, reduces pressure losses, and simplifies the
integration of the thermal management subsystem. Locating the fuel-cell stack close to the hydrogen tank additionally
reduces system complexity and improves packaging efficiency.
The high-voltage DC bus and associated power electronics were also integrated within the central fuselage. Since
the propulsion motors are distributed across the canard, main wing, and tail surfaces, a centralized power distribution
architecture minimizes the number of power conversion stages while simplifying fault detection and electrical isolation.
The six EMRAX 348 electric motors are installed directly within the tilt-duct assemblies. This configuration
eliminates the need for mechanical transmissions, gearboxes, and long rotating shafts, thereby reducing mechanical
losses and improving overall system reliability. Positioning the motors at the propulsion units also minimizes low-
voltage, high-current motor cable lengths and simplifies thermal integration with the surrounding airflow.
Overall, the adopted arrangement concentrates the heaviest energy-generation and storage components near the
aircraft center of gravity while distributing only the propulsion units to the lifting surfaces. This architecture reduces
wiring complexity, limits structural penalties, and improves both maintainability and fault tolerance.

8.2
Ducts CAD Model
The integration of the enclosing circular duct performs three vital functions in system performance: it provides
thrust augmentation of approximately 25% to 35% compared to a free rotor due to the pressure distribution at the
inlet lip; it executes static pressure recovery along the diffuser; and it confines the flow, severely mitigating blade
tip vortices. The duct is designed to operate efficiently under vertical flight conditions (VTOL), where axial flow
predominates, and in horizontal cruise flight at a speed of 85 m/s.
The CAD modeling of the duct assemblies was driven by strict aerodynamic and geometric continuity requirements
to ensure optimal thrust augmentation and minimal drag during cruise. The macro-geometry of the completed duct
dictates a maximum outer diameter of 2.275 m and a total axial height of 1.100 m.
The inlet lip profile was modeled in the CAD software using spline curves with a G2 geometric continuity constraint.
This methodological requirement is critical to prevent surface curvature discontinuities that would otherwise cause
local peaks in the static pressure coefficient (Cp) and, consequently, induce premature boundary layer transition or
flow separation at the inlet. The maximum thickness of the lip was strategically positioned between 15% and 20% of
the chord, and the lip radius was defined at 4.7% of the internal diameter to optimize the suction pressure distribution
before the throat, a configuration that maximizes the experimental figure of merit as validated by Abrego and Bulaga
[21].
The outer wall of the duct was designed with a thin, supercritical aerodynamic contour, strictly maintaining a
thickness-to-chord ratio of t/c ≤6%. The geometry incorporates a severely sharp trailing edge to suppress base
aerodynamic wake generation and minimize parasitic drag during the horizontal cruise phase.
Internally, the diffuser’s semi-opening angle was rigidly constrained to approximately 4◦, and the expansion ratio
was set to 1.097. This expansion ratio falls perfectly within the optimal 1.05 to 1.15 range established by Zhang
and Barakos [22]. This geometric limit was enforced in the CAD model to prevent the internal expansion angle from
approaching the critical 7◦to 8◦threshold, at which massive adverse pressure gradients and complete flow separation
would occur, nullifying the duct’s pressure recovery benefits.

Emissions & Noise
To rigorously assess the environmental profile of the proposed hybrid eVTOL aircraft, this chapter evaluates
its lifecycle Global Warming Potential (GWP) and acoustic footprint. Given that the propulsion system operates
with zero direct emissions during the flight phase [24], the environmental assessment shifts toward a cradle-to-grave
framework, capturing the upstream carbon debt and the localized operational impacts [25, 26].
10.1
Emissions for Production and Replacement
The primary environmental trade-off of hybrid-electric configurations lies in the shifting of emissions from the
operational phase to the material extraction and manufacturing stages. Therefore, quantifying the production and
replacement footprints is critical to determining the true net sustainability of the aircraft. The analysis is framed
around the following technical boundaries:
• System Boundaries (LCA): Due to the lack of consolidated aerospace-grade data regarding the manufacturing
and End-of-Life (EOL) decommissioning of novel composite airframes, the scope of this assessment is strictly
delimited to the primary energy storage and conversion architectures [25, 26], namely, the Lithium-Sulfur (Li-S)
battery pack and the Proton Exchange Membrane Fuel Cell (PEMFC).
• Technological Constraints: While the hybrid architecture mitigates the low specific energy constraints in-
herent to pure battery systems [27], the production of high-power electrical components and complex thermal
management systems [28] introduces a significant initial environmental burden that must be amortized over
the aircraft’s commercial operation.
• Amortization and Lifespan Metrics: Because these energy systems degrade over time due to cyclic thermal
and electrical stress, their manufacturing emissions cannot be treated as a permanent capital expense. How-
ever, different technologies exhibit distinct degradation mechanisms and operational lifetimes. Therefore, the
manufacturing emissions associated with each subsystem are amortized individually according to their respec-
tive useful lives. For this study, a useful life of 1000 cycles is assumed for the Lithium-Sulfur (Li-S) battery
pack. For the Proton Exchange Membrane Fuel Cell (PEMFC) system, considering an estimated lifespan of
5000 flight hours and an average flight duration of 35 minutes, it is assumed to operate for approximately 8571
cycles. This approach provides a more realistic emission-per-flight allocation and better reflects the long-term
durability advantages of fuel cell technology.

The Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) applied to this hybrid UAM configuration highlights that direct zero-emission
flight does not automatically guarantee absolute environmental sustainability. In isolated or fossil-dependent energy
ecosystems, such as the Balearic Islands, the operational carbon intensity dramatically reshapes the aircraft’s ecological
profile.
When realistic component lifetimes are considered, the manufacturing contribution remains relatively low. Al-
though the energy systems require significant upfront carbon investments, evaluating the manufacturing footprint
over the estimated operational flights limits the amortization penalty. As a result, the total manufacturing burden
allocated to each flight represents 3.321 kgCO2-eq/flight.
Using the local Balearic electricity grid (0.500 kgCO2-eq/kWh), battery recharging contributes approximately
10.100 kgCO2-eq per flight. The hydrogen production pathway then becomes one of the dominant factors influencing
overall lifecycle emissions. Green hydrogen produced via electrolysis contributes approximately 1.553 kgCO2-eq per
flight, whereas grey hydrogen produced through natural gas reforming contributes approximately 17.084 kgCO2-eq
per flight.
Consequently, the total lifecycle footprint reaches 14.974 kgCO2-eq/flight for the green hydrogen scenario and
30.505 kgCO2-eq/flight for the grey hydrogen scenario. This represents an increase of approximately 104% when
fossil-derived hydrogen is utilized.
These results demonstrate that the environmental performance of hybrid hydrogen-electric eVTOL aircraft is
driven predominantly by the carbon intensity of the supporting energy infrastructure.
Therefore, the successful
deployment of such aircraft in insular regions depends not only on advances in vehicle technology but also on the
availability of low-carbon electricity and dedicated green hydrogen production pathways.

10.2.3
Effect of Ducted Rotors on Noise Reduction
The selected tilt-duct architecture provides several inherent aeroacoustic advantages compared with equivalent
open-rotor configurations. The primary benefit arises from the suppression of blade-tip vortex formation. In con-
ventional propellers, the pressure difference between the pressure and suction sides of the blade generates strong tip
vortices that contribute significantly to broadband noise production. The presence of the duct limits this cross-flow
motion, which literature indicates can reduce the broadband noise component by 8 to 10 dB during approach phases
[36]. In this case, doing a vortex noise analysis is highly complex; traditional semi-empirical models, like the one de-
fined by Schlegel (1966), fail to provide useful insights as they neglect duct acoustic cut-off frequencies and boundary
layer ingestion (BLI) effects.
Additionally, the duct acts as a partial acoustic shield, attenuating the direct propagation of rotor-generated
pressure fluctuations toward external observers. Acoustic simulations of integrated urban ducted systems show that
a portion of the acoustic energy is reflected, absorbed, or redirected by the duct geometry, which can contribute to
an overall noise level reduction outside the propulsion system.
Furthermore, the duct also enables the use of smaller rotor diameters operating at lower tip speeds for a given thrust
requirement due to the pressure-recovery effects generated inside the shroud. Since aerodynamic noise scales strongly
with blade tip velocity following the 50 log10(N2/N1) scaling law, even a moderate 20% reduction in rotational speed
produces a noticeable 4 dB acoustic benefit (growing to 8 dB at a 30% speed reduction).
These characteristics explain why ducted-fan concepts such as the Lilium Jet and Bell Nexus have consistently
been proposed as low-noise solutions for urban air mobility applications. Although the additional structure introduces
mass and drag penalties, the resulting reduction in community noise represents a significant advantage for operations
near populated areas where public acceptance is a critical design driver.
10.2.4
Potential Noise Mitigation Strategies
There are no internationally finalized ICAO noise certification standards for UAM and eVTOL aircraft, and EASA
is currently addressing noise requirements on an individual, case-by-case basis. In this section, only loudness was
studied in a semi-empirical way. However, it is of extreme importance to reduce acoustic pollution as much as possible.
To achieve this, future analyses must evaluate sound frequency alongside loudness. Human auditory perception varies
significantly across different frequencies, meaning that the pitch and tonal characteristics of eVTOL rotors can greatly
impact public annoyance, even at lower decibel levels.
Although the predicted noise levels exceed the desired community-noise target during maximum-load operation,
several mitigation measures may be considered in future design iterations. The primary objective of these strategies
is to reduce ground-level sound pressure levels during take-off and transition, which represent the acoustically critical
phases of the mission.
A promising approach consists of implementing noise-aware flight control laws. Adaptive rotor-speed scheduling
can minimize rotor RPM whenever full thrust is unnecessary, while optimized transition maneuvers can reduce the
duration of high-power operating conditions. Similarly, trajectory optimization may be used to increase separation
from populated areas during the noisiest phases of flight.
Additional reductions may be achieved through passive acoustic treatments. Acoustic liners integrated into the
duct structure can attenuate tonal pressure fluctuations before they radiate to the surrounding environment, while
localized fuselage insulation can improve passenger comfort by reducing the transmission of rotor-induced noise and
58

vibration into the cabin.
Ultimately, the results indicate that acoustic performance is strongly influenced by the relatively high rotor tip
speed required during maximum-load operation. Future design refinements should therefore focus on reducing rotor
rotational speed while combining advanced flight-control strategies and acoustic treatments to further mitigate the
environmental impact of the vehicle.

The life-cycle cost (LCC) of an aircraft can be divided into three main categories: research, development, test,
and evaluation (RDT&E) costs; acquisition cost (A); and operation and maintenance (O&M) costs [8].
CLCC = CRDT &E + CA + CO&M
(11.1)
For this study, it was decided to consider the first two components (RDT&E and A), which, when added together,
represent the aircraft’s selling price. We therefore chose not to carry out a detailed assessment of the operation and
maintenance (O&M) costs, which consist of expenses related in this case with electricity and hydrogen, oils and
lubricants, as well as those associated with the crew and maintenance team.
For this study, the primary focus of the engineering cost model is placed on the first two components (RDT&E
and A), which, when added together, determine the aircraft’s manufacturing selling price. A bottom-up, detailed
assessment of operational and maintenance (O&M) variables (such as specific crew salaries, detailed insurance
premiums, and exact hangar fees) is outside the immediate scope of the structural production phase. However, a
macro-level estimation of key O&M expenses (including fuel, battery lifecycles, and routine maintenance) is introduced
later in Subsection 11.2.1 solely to gauge the final concept’s commercial feasibility and per-passenger ticketing viability.

To evaluate the economic feasibility of the proposed concept, its mass-production unit cost was benchmarked

18,181 flights over its lifespan, translating to a baseline asset amortization of $275.01 per flight, or $45.84 per
passenger.
However, the actual operational expenditure must account for critical recurring variables. As a preliminary O&M
approximation, it is estimated that the batteries will need to be replaced at least every 1000 flight hours. Furthermore,
additional recurring expenses (including hydrogen fuel, electricity, and routine maintenance) are projected to double
the base amortization cost of the asset. Taking these operational variables into account, the total reference cost per
passenger amounts to approximately $91, providing a baseline for commercial viability.
Ultimately, even when applying a robust 50% profit margin for service implementation, the final ticket price per
passenger ($136.5) remains significantly below initial estimates, provided the concept successfully transitions into

Conclusion
This report developed and assessed a conceptual proposal for a premium Urban Air Mobility system for inter-island
transportation in the Balearic Archipelago. The market and operational analyses confirmed that the Ibiza-Palma de
Mallorca and Palma de Mallorca-Menorca routes represent a relevant use case for a fast, low-noise, and low-emission
VTOL service.
The main achievement of the work was the definition of a technically plausible aircraft concept whose design
point remains within the feasible design space as per the latest run on the Aircraft Design Tool.
The selected
tilt-duct configuration satisfies the reference mission while remaining compatible with the main operational and
regulatory constraints considered throughout the report. The aircraft is able to perform the mission with one pilot
and six passengers, while preserving a configuration that is consistent with distributed electric propulsion and future
low-emission aviation objectives.
A central outcome of the project is the adoption of a hybrid electric-fuel-cell architecture. Although a fully electric
aircraft was investigated, its feasibility was found to depend strongly on optimistic battery assumptions. The retained
solution combines a Li-S battery pack for VTOL, hover, and transient power with a hydrogen PEMFC subsystem
for cruise and climb. This architecture remains aligned with the initial objective of eliminating direct in-flight CO2
emissions. The remaining environmental impacts are shifted mainly to hydrogen production, energy acquisition, and
battery replacement, which surpasses the aviation domain to the economical and technological ones.
The hybrid architecture also showed promising scalability. The estimated additional hydrogen-storage mass for
range extension is approximately 2.5 kg per extra 10 km of cruise range under the assumptions used in the report.
This value is preliminary and will likely increase when secondary electrical loads, integration penalties, and refined
reserves are included, but it still indicates one of the strongest advantages of the hybrid solution compared with a
purely battery-based aircraft.
The noise and emissions studies also support the relevance of the concept. The estimated noise levels are within
a promising range for a UAM aircraft, although the current assessment relies on simplified approximations and
conventional rotor assumptions. Since the final aircraft uses ducted rotors, the actual acoustic behavior may differ
and could potentially improve if the duct geometry is properly optimized. This reinforces the need for a dedicated
aeroacoustic study in future iterations.
The cost analysis produced another important conclusion. Initial unit costs are high, as expected for a first-of-
a-kind aircraft using historical cost-estimating relationships, but the cost per passenger becomes more competitive
when larger production volumes and high utilization are considered. The results suggest that the concept may not
need to remain exclusively in a luxury segment and could potentially target a medium-high market segment, provided
that operating costs, infrastructure costs, and maintenance requirements remain controlled.
The main limitation of the current design is geometric compactness. Although the tilt-duct architecture offers
advantages in safety and integration, the resulting aircraft still has significant dimensions, particularly because of
the wing and duct arrangement required to satisfy the mission and VTOL power requirements. This means that
the initial objective of a highly compact VTOL aircraft was only partially achieved, and compatibility with existing
heliports or constrained vertiport infrastructure must be studied further.
Overall, the aircraft can perform the reference mission under the assumptions adopted in this conceptual design.

The project therefore supports the feasibility of a hybrid tilt-duct UAM aircraft for Balearic inter-island transport,
while also identifying the main areas where higher-fidelity analysis is required before the concept can be considered
mature.
12.1
Future Work
The difficulties encountered during this conceptual development provide a clear direction for future work. The
first priority should be to improve the aerodynamic and propulsive fidelity of the design. The ducted rotors should
be assessed with higher-order aerodynamic tools, including CFD, to verify hover performance, transition behavior,
rotor–duct interaction, tip-clearance effects, and acoustic emissions. The rotor geometry, blade profiles and duct
shape should then be optimized together rather than treated as independent components.
A second priority is structural and mass validation. Several subsystem masses were necessarily estimated using
simplified methods, and these assumptions should be replaced by preliminary structural sizing. The wing, canard,
tail, fuselage, ducts, tilting mechanisms, and landing-gear attachments require dedicated analysis to confirm that the
assumed mass distribution is realistic. Particular attention should be given to the concentrated loads introduced by
the tilt-duct units.
The hybrid powertrain also requires further refinement. Future work should define the hydrogen tank geometry,
pressure-vessel installation, fuel-cell cooling system, battery C-rate capability, high-voltage protection, wiring layout,
and failure-isolation strategy in greater detail. A mission-level energy-management simulation should also be imple-
mented to evaluate battery state of charge, fuel-cell operating points, reserve margins, and off-design scenarios such
as diversions, missed approaches, or degraded propulsion operation.
The economic and environmental assessments should also be expanded. The cost model should include operation
and maintenance, hydrogen or electricity supply, infrastructure, crew, battery replacement, and fleet utilization. These
factors are essential to estimate the real unit cost, cost per passenger, and market positioning of the aircraft. The
environmental analysis should also be extended to include hydrogen production, battery manufacturing, component
replacement, and end-of-life treatment.
Finally, future configuration studies should investigate whether a smaller aircraft could provide a better balance
between compactness, cost, and operational flexibility. One possible direction is to reduce the capacity from six
passengers to four passengers, which could reduce the overall dimensions, simplify the fuselage, and potentially
remove the canard as a structural dependency. Alternative propulsion layouts should also be considered, such as four
ducts or two larger and more powerful ducted units mounted primarily on the main wing. These alternatives emerged
from the main design challenges identified during the project and may lead to a more compact and operationally
flexible second iteration of the concept.

A.2
Energy Budget Estimation Methodology
This section presents the analytical framework employed to estimate the total energy consumption of the hybrid-
electric aircraft across all phases of a representative mission profile. The mission is decomposed into five distinct
segments: hover, vertical take-off and landing (VTOL), climb, cruise, and descent (with regenerative energy recovery).
Each segment is analysed independently, and the results are subsequently summed to yield the total energy budget.
A.2.1
Hover and VTOL Phase
Lift and Thrust Requirements
For steady, level hover, the total lift generated by the rotor system must equal the aircraft weight:
Lhover = W
(A.1)
A structural and aerodynamic margin of 20% is applied to define the maximum lift condition:
Lmax = 1.2 Lhover
(A.2)
Assuming the thrust is distributed equally among the n rotors, the thrust per rotor in hover and at maximum
load is, respectively:
Thover = Lhover
n
(A.3)
Tmax = Lmax

(A.4)

Arotor operating in air of density ρ, the ideal power associated with a given thrust T is:
Pideal =
s
T 3
2ρArotor
(A.5)
Accordingly, the total ideal power for the full rotor system in hover and at maximum thrust conditions is:
Phover, ideal = n

2ρArotor
(A.6)
Pmax, ideal = n
s
T 3max

(A.7)
where ρ is evaluated at sea level under International Standard Atmosphere (ISA) conditions, representing the worst-
case scenario for rotor efficiency.

Real Power with Safety and Non-ideality Margins
A combined correction factor k is applied to account for a safety margin and for aerodynamic non-idealities (tip
losses, induced losses, blade profile drag, etc.):
Phover, real = k Phover, ideal
(A.8)
Pmax, real = k Pmax, ideal
(A.9)
Energy Consumed in Hover and VTOL
The energy consumed in each phase is the product of the (real) power demand and the duration of that phase.
Both the take-off and landing VTOL segments are accounted for (factor of 2), and similarly both hover segments:
Ehover = 2 Phover, real · thover
(A.10)
EVTOL = 2 Pmax, real · tVTOL
(A.11)
A.2.2
Cruise Phase
During level cruise, the required thrust equals the aerodynamic drag:
Tcruise = D = 1
2ρcruise v2
cruise S CD
(A.12)
where ρcruise is the air density at cruise altitude, vcruise is the true airspeed, S is the reference wing area, and CD
is the drag coefficient. The shaft power delivered to the propulsion system, accounting for the propulsive efficiency
ηprop, is:
Pcruise = D vcruise
ηprop
(A.13)
The energy consumed over the cruise segment of duration tcruise is:
Ecruise = Pcruise · tcruise
(A.14)
A.2.3
Climb Phase
During the climb segment, power must overcome both aerodynamic drag and the rate of change of gravitational
potential energy. Denoting the climb angle as γ and the climb true airspeed as vclimb, the gravitational and drag
power components are:
Pgrav = m g vclimb sin γ
(A.15)
Pdrag = 1
2ρclimb v3
climb S CD
(A.16)

where ρclimb is the average air density over the climb segment. The total mechanical power required, corrected for
propulsive efficiency, is:
Pclimb = Pgrav + Pdrag
ηprop
(A.17)
The energy consumed during the climb phase of duration tclimb is:
Eclimb = Pclimb · tclimb
(A.18)
A.2.4
Descent Phase with Regenerative Energy Recovery
During a powered glide descent at angle γ and airspeed vdesc, gravitational power exceeds drag power, yielding a
net surplus that can be partially recovered through regenerative braking:
Pgrav, desc = m g vdesc sin γ
(A.19)
Pdrag, desc = 1
2ρdesc v3
desc S CD
(A.20)
The net power available for regeneration (a negative value, indicating energy returned to the system) is:
Pdesc = Pdrag, desc −Pgrav, desc
(A.21)
Applying the regeneration efficiency ηreg, the recovered energy over the descent segment of duration tdesc is:
Ereg, desc = ηreg · Pdesc · tdesc
(A.22)
Since Pdesc < 0, Ereg, desc is negative, representing energy fed back into the storage system.
A.2.5
Total Mission Energy
The total electrical energy demand for the complete mission profile is the algebraic sum of the contributions from
each segment:
Etotal = Ehover + EVTOL + Eclimb + Ecruise + Ereg, desc
(A.23)
This total constitutes the minimum energy capacity that the hybrid-electric energy storage system must supply, and
serves as the primary sizing criterion for the battery and/or fuel-cell components of the hybrid subsystem.
